The hype is everywhere and it's <a href="https://trends.google.com/explore?q=AI&date=today%205-y&geo=US">growing</a>. Blogs are pivoting almost entirely into discussing artificial intelligence ad nauseum, from engineering the <a href="https://moderndata101.substack.com/">modern data stack</a> with AI in mind to <a href="https://www.oneusefulthing.org/">professors of innovation</a> talking about this next big thing and a thousand other blogs. Or is it a million? With AI seemingly lowering the barrier to entry to just about everything, from <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12308071/">biotech</a>, <a href="https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/ai-in-leadership">business management</a>, <a href="https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/what-the-fastest-growing-tools-reveal-about-how-software-is-being-built/">programming and development</a> with <a href="https://github.com/features/copilot">higher reported levels of job satisfaction</a> (<a href="https://www.featheranalytics.dev/talks/Lies,%20Damned%20Lies,%20and%20Statistics.pdf">statistics are fun</a>), <a href="https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/china-grabs-top-humanoid-robot-spots">robotics</a>, <a href="https://www.composer.trade/">trading</a>, and more. I would venture to say that if you can think of it, somewhere, someone is trying to bootstrap a startup to shove AI into something. 

I am always interested in seeing how economic concepts reveal themselves. From <a href="https://www.economicforces.xyz/p/an-equilibrium-perspective-on-ai">equilibrium</a> to <a href="https://www.economicforces.xyz/p/gdp-and-living-standards">living standards</a> (no, this post is not about the GDP debate) to <a href="https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/ai-is-power-hungry">externalities</a> to <a href="https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/why-does-everyone-hate-ai">expectations</a>, there is plenty of discussion on the possible potential impacts of AI on tomorrow and beyond. 

I have used these tools since <a href="https://www.reuters.com/technology/microsoft-backed-openai-starts-release-powerful-ai-known-gpt-4-2023-03-14/">GPT-4</a> and, through that aforementioned barrier lowering, these tools have certainly helped me develop my skill set. These days I exclusively use Claude Code. Living in the terminal is the easiest way for me to use AI for what I use it for the most.

What this post covers, with some (AI-generated) interactive charts, is an intersection that I am particularly interested in. This post primarily stems from two things. The first is my own personal coming-of-age as the world economy recovered from the Great Finacial Crisis, and the companies that grew to some level of dominance such as AirBnb and Uber. The second is my work experience in technology, specifically on the data side of things. 

Through my own use, and frustrations, I have never lost my skepticism. What *is* the end goal of these companies? What is their incentive?

**Outline — replace with prose.**

- Budget-tier models are floored near {FIGURES.budgetFloor} per million tokens; frontier models hold near {`$${COST_FLOOR.frontierStableLow}–${COST_FLOOR.frontierStableHigh}`}.
- {COST_FLOOR.note}
- Both halves of the floor are now reached rather than predicted. The budget band has been touched and not breached, and the model that touches it is open weights on commodity hosting rather than a lab flagship. Eight models from five providers on two continents sit inside the frontier band, which is three dollars wide.
- The {FIGURES.deflationFactor} fall is a cheapest-available-SKU chain with capability held fixed at neither end, so it is not the price of a constant level of capability falling that far. Name which quantity it measures before saying anything about whether it can repeat.
- Where the slowdown is actually visible: twelve SKUs held their exact rates from June to August, the next round of savings is expected from routing, caching and batching rather than per-token cuts, and several of the cheapest headline rates carry published expiry dates — introductory pricing with a scheduled step-up, not a deflating commodity.

<Fragment slot="step-2">

**Outline — replace with prose.**

- Revenue is price times quantity, so {FIGURES.priceDrop} off price alongside {FIGURES.revenueGrowth} revenue implies volume grew by roughly 240x. That is what the two inputs jointly imply, not a measurement — the corpus declines to use this division for volume, because it sets two firms' revenue against an enterprise-category spend figure over different years on different bases.
- What is measured instead is {FIGURES.statedVolume} over 24 months, from tokens processed as disclosed at three consecutive Google I/O keynotes. Disclosed rather than inferred, one firm, composition not held fixed across a window in which image, video and audio tokens became a large share, and billed and unbilled volume not separated — so it cannot be multiplied by a price to recover revenue.
- The two figures disagree, and the disagreement is the substance of this step. The elasticity the divided aggregates imply is {FIGURES.impliedElasticity}, far past the backfire threshold. The one published estimate — Demirer, Fradkin, Tadelis and Peng, NBER Working Paper 34608 — puts the short-run price elasticity just above one in magnitude.
- The measured figure is also not the parameter the argument needs, and that cuts the other way from how it first reads. It is identified from price variation across providers serving the same model, so it recovers a cross-host substitution elasticity and bounds the market-level parameter from above. The market-level figure a Jevons claim is about is probably below one and has never been estimated by anyone. No point estimate or standard error appears in the reachable version of the paper.
- Do not write that each cut raised total spending. Apply the measured elasticity to the observed price path and the price channel alone accounts for a volume rise on the order of 7-8x against the {FIGURES.statedVolume} observed. The paper's own authors conclude that short-run Jevons is unlikely to operate at the market level.
- **Chart shows:** the callout naming the divergence and the implied elasticity — implied is the word that has to survive into the prose.

</Fragment>

## Who supplies this

**Outline — replace with prose.**

- {FIGURES.capex} guided for 2026 capacity — guidance, not committed spend, against actuals of roughly 231 billion dollars in 2024 and 361 billion in 2025. Guidance in this cycle has been revised up every quarter, and Microsoft reports fiscal years while the other three report calendar years, so the combined figure mixes bases.
- Every share here needs its date read with it and none is current. Anthropic's enterprise API share ({SUPPLY_SIDE.anthropicEnterpriseApiSharePct}%) is the December 2025 survey, the most recent that exists. ChatGPT's consumer share ({SUPPLY_SIDE.chatgptConsumerSharePct}%) is Similarweb web-visit share for May 2026, down about 25 points year over year, and cuts of the same data disagree by roughly 7 points depending on the denominator. Google's routing share ({SUPPLY_SIDE.googleRoutingSharePct}%) is a 2025 figure that could not be re-sourced for 2026 at all.
- {SUPPLY_SIDE.lockInConcernedPct}% are concerned about lock-in; only {FIGURES.couldSwitch} could actually switch today. Use the direction and nothing more: this is a vendor-run survey with no published sampling frame or question wording, asking about cloud and end-user computing generally rather than about AI.
- Open-weight models trail the frontier by {SUPPLY_SIDE.openWeightLagMonths} on the early-2026 figure. Measured in August the gap is three index points to the best open weights outright, ten points to the best a large buyer can deploy without negotiating a license, and thirty-nine points for a buyer who cannot deploy Chinese-origin weights at all — no credible substitution threat, and no effective floor.
- Identical weights get hosted at up to a {SUPPLY_SIDE.hostingSpreadForIdenticalWeights} spread. That range spans three open-weight models rather than bounding one, and it compresses toward 2x on newer models with fewer hosts — backwards from what live price competition would predict, which points at stale unrepriced SKUs at premium providers rather than at rivalry.

## The Thread

**Outline — replace with prose.**

- PPF sets the tradeoff → the window-quality equilibrium shows the gain from a bigger window is sublinear rather than proportional → the budget constraint relaxes as price falls → Jevons describes what happened after it relaxed without explaining why.
- That last link is the one to get right. The collection's own falsification criteria say its first call was wrong: capability and workflow adoption did most of the causal work, and the price channel accounts for 7-8x of the {FIGURES.statedVolume} move. The price fall was necessary rather than sufficient.
- Price fell {FIGURES.priceDrop} → the constraint loosened → usage grew far past the price drop → total spend grew {FIGURES.revenueGrowth} anyway. Add the part nobody wrote in advance: more than 85 percent of agentic tokens became cache reads, so cost per unit of agentic work fell by close to an order of magnitude with no fall in token counts. That strengthens the volume claim and weakens the revenue claim.

## Where this breaks

**Outline — replace with prose.**

- The conditions that would prove this story wrong, not just make it less tidy — and they are scored now, not merely listed. Three were stated in June, a fourth was added in August, and each carries its own verdict below.
- Two things the list cannot carry on its own. A fifth condition, geopolitical bifurcation of the model supply chain, belongs in the set and cannot be scored from market-internal evidence at all. And what actually happened is a condition nobody wrote in advance: agentic tokens became overwhelmingly cache reads, which strengthens the volume claim and weakens the revenue claim — the opposite pairing from what the third condition contemplated.
- {JEVONS.newProductProblem}
